The finding

Static, rule-based skill scanners barely fire — on known attacks and on novel disguised behaviour alike (3–16% recall). LLM-backed scanning detects substantially more — whether a commercial product (Snyk) or a cheap one-shot open-weight baseline — but at a real false-positive cost (15–25% on dual-use). The dividing line is the approach (signatures vs semantic reading), not the vendor. Scope: pre-execution static skill-file analysis.

On the small set of real wild samples the gap is not yet statistically resolvable (n=4–5, overlapping intervals) — the strong claim rests on known + reconstructed-novel behaviour, with wild as supporting evidence we are growing.

Not self-recognition: the best detector (phi-4) generated none of the test corpus and is scored cross-family; the in-set model (Qwen) scores worst on its own generated samples; and on benign skills run through the same six generators + same defang (the generated-benign control), phi-4/Qwen flag 0% — they key on malice, not on machine-generated style. LLM baselines are open-weight controls, not products. SkillSpector & Cisco are graded on their static layer only — their own +llm modes could not be evaluated (open-weight endpoint limits; a frontier pass was ~100% blocked by the provider's prompt-injection gateway), a backend-availability limit, not a verdict on their LLM modes (see Methodology).
